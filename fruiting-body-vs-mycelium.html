Fruiting Body vs Mycelium Extract

The single most important distinction in mushroom extracts — and the one most labels hope you won't ask about.

HerbChina Extracts · Resources

Two products can both be called "reishi extract" while containing almost nothing in common. The difference is what part of the organism was extracted — and most buyers never think to ask.

The three things sold as "mushroom extract"

  • Fruiting body extract — extracted from the actual mushroom (the cap and stem). This is the traditional medicinal material, and where beta-glucans and triterpenoids are concentrated.
  • Pure mycelium extract — extracted from the root-like mycelial network, grown in liquid culture without grain. A legitimate material with its own profile, but different from fruiting body.
  • Mycelium on grain — mycelium grown on rice, oats, or sorghum, then ground up with the grain still in it. The result can be mostly starch, with a mushroom label on the front.

Why beta-glucan is the marker that matters

Beta-glucans are the best-studied active compounds in functional mushrooms. A serious supplier states the beta-glucan percentage on the spec sheet. What many don't volunteer is the alpha-glucan (starch) content alongside it — high alpha-glucan is the fingerprint of grain filler.

Rule of thumb: if a "mushroom extract" lists polysaccharides but won't break out beta-glucan separately, treat the number with suspicion. Polysaccharide tests count starch too.

Four questions to ask any supplier

  1. What was extracted — fruiting body, mycelium, or mycelium on grain? The answer should be one thing, stated plainly, not "a proprietary blend."
  2. What is the beta-glucan content, and the alpha-glucan content? Both numbers. If they only know one, they haven't tested properly.
  3. What test method was used? Beta-glucan should be measured by a recognized method — ask which one.
  4. Can I see a batch-specific COA? With the species' Latin name, the source material declared, and the glucan numbers printed on it.

How grain filler shows up on a COA

A COA will rarely say "this product contains grain filler." Instead, the filler shows up indirectly, in how the numbers are reported and what is left unsaid. Read these lines closely:

  • Alpha-glucan or starch. Pure fruiting body and pure mycelium extracts contain very little alpha-glucan. A high alpha-glucan figure, or an explicitly reported "starch" value, points to residual grain substrate in a mycelium-on-grain product. Some suppliers report total polysaccharides and never break them into beta-glucan and alpha-glucan fractions; that single blended number hides the split.
  • Vague "polysaccharides." A line reading "polysaccharides: 30%" tells you nothing about whether the 30% is beta-glucan or grain starch. Ask for the beta-glucan method (for example, a validated enzymatic method) and the specific figure, not a summed total.
  • Missing source-material declaration. A COA should state the part used: fruiting body, mycelium, or mycelium grown on grain. When that line is absent, the reader cannot interpret the rest of the document. Absence is itself a signal.
  • No lot-level link to raw material. Batch or lot numbers that do not trace back to a named raw-material lot make it hard to know what was actually extracted.

None of these lines proves a problem on its own. Together, they tell you how much the supplier is willing to disclose.

What "dual extract" and "full spectrum" actually mean

These terms describe a process, not a guaranteed outcome. "Dual extract" normally means the material was extracted with both water and a food-grade solvent such as ethanol, which pulls out both water-soluble and alcohol-soluble compounds. That is a real and useful process — but the term does not tell you the ratios, temperatures, or how the two extracts were combined.

"Full spectrum" is looser. It usually implies the extract retains a broad range of naturally occurring compounds rather than a single isolated marker. It does not specify which spectrum, at what concentration, or compared to what reference.

When you see either term, ask:

  • What solvents and temperatures were used, and in what order?
  • Is the final product a blend of the two extracts, and at what ratio?
  • What is the beta-glucan figure, and by which method?
  • Was the starting material fruiting body, mycelium, or mycelium-on-grain?

Treat both terms as an invitation to ask, not as a specification.

Price signals: what unusually cheap usually means

Price gaps between suppliers are normal, but very large gaps deserve a question rather than an accusation. Legitimate cost drivers include the raw material itself (fruiting bodies are slower and costlier to produce than grain-grown mycelium), extraction yield, solvent and energy costs, batch size, and how much testing the supplier commissions. A cheaper product may simply be mycelium-on-grain extract sold without saying so, or a lower-concentration extract diluted to a target number. It may also be a genuine efficiency gain. You cannot tell from price alone; you can tell by asking for the beta-glucan method, the source-material declaration, and a lot-specific COA. If those answers are clear and consistent, the price may be fair. If they are vague, the price is not the only thing that is unclear.

Before your first order: a practical checklist

  1. Request a sample and a lot-specific COA, not a generic spec sheet.
  2. Confirm the source-material declaration: fruiting body, mycelium, or mycelium-on-grain.
  3. Ask for the beta-glucan figure and the test method used to obtain it.
  4. Check whether alpha-glucan or starch is reported separately.
  5. Verify the lot number on the COA matches the sample and the quoted batch.
  6. Start with a small trial lot before committing to volume, and re-test on receipt if the application warrants it.

These steps cost little and surface most of the questions that matter before money changes hands.
