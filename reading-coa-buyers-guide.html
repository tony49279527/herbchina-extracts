How to Read a Botanical Extract COA

A practical, section-by-section walkthrough of the Certificate of Analysis — what each part means, where documents commonly fall short, and how to verify what you are being told.

HerbChina Extracts · Resources

If you buy botanical or mushroom extracts for supplement, food, or cosmetic products, the Certificate of Analysis (COA) sits at the center of your quality documentation. It is what your QA team reviews before releasing an incoming shipment, what you may need to show a regulator, and one of the few documents that can confirm whether the material in the drum matches what you ordered.

But many buyers treat the COA as a box to tick rather than a document to read. This guide explains how to read a botanical extract COA in practical terms, what each section means, where documents commonly fall short, and how to verify what you are being told. The goal is not to make you a lab chemist. It is to help you ask better questions and catch problems before they reach your production line.

Short on time? Start with our quick-start guide, How to Read a Botanical Extract COA — this article is the deep dive: test methods, verification steps, and a full buyer checklist.

What a COA Is, and What It Is Not

A Certificate of Analysis is a batch-specific record of test results. It states what was tested, what method was used, what the result was, and who approved it. Ideally, it also names the specification the batch was tested against, so you can see whether the result passes or fails.

A COA is not a marketing sheet. A specification sheet, product brochure, or "technical data sheet" often describes what a product can meet or typically meets. That is useful for early screening, but it is not evidence about a specific batch.

A COA is also only valid for the batch it names. If you receive a COA from last year, or one that references a different lot number than the material in your warehouse, it tells you nothing about your shipment. Every batch should have its own COA, and the batch number on the COA should match the batch number on the drum, label, and packing list.

Anatomy of a Botanical Extract COA

COAs vary in layout, but most well-constructed documents contain the following sections. Use this as a checklist when you read one.

Product Identity

This section should clearly state:

  • Botanical name (genus and species), ideally with the common name
  • Plant part used (root, leaf, fruit, mycelium, fruiting body, etc.)
  • Extract ratio (for example, 10:1) or standardization statement
  • Extraction solvent (water, ethanol, or another solvent)

These details matter because one common name can hide different species with different chemistry: "ginseng" could be Panax ginseng or Panax quinquefolius, and "reishi" could be fruiting body or mycelium. Without species and part on the COA, you cannot confirm you received what you ordered.

Appearance and Organoleptic Specifications

This section describes color, form (powder, granule, extract paste), odor, and sometimes taste. These are subjective attributes, but they still matter: if a COA states "fine brown powder" and you receive a sticky dark paste, that is a discrepancy worth flagging. Organoleptic specs never substitute for analytical testing, but they are a useful first-line sanity check.

Active Marker Assay and the Method Behind It

This is usually the section buyers care about most. Many extracts are standardized to a marker compound — a polysaccharide, flavonoid, or other characteristic constituent — and the COA should state:

  • Which marker was assayed
  • The result, expressed as a percentage or another unit
  • The analytical method used

Common methods you will see include:

  • HPLC (High-Performance Liquid Chromatography): Separates and quantifies individual compounds. It is generally the more specific method because it can distinguish closely related molecules.
  • UV-Vis (Ultraviolet-Visible Spectroscopy): Measures absorbance at a specific wavelength. It is common for certain compound classes but can be less specific, since other substances may absorb at the same wavelength.
  • GC (Gas Chromatography): Used for volatile compounds, fatty acids, and residual solvents, often paired with detectors such as FID or MS.
  • Titration: A wet-chemistry method that measures a compound by reacting it with a reagent of known concentration, appropriate for certain acids and other measurable constituents.

The method matters because two labs can report different results for the same material using different methods — a result is only meaningful when you know how it was obtained. If the COA just says "HPLC", it is reasonable to ask for the method reference.

Moisture and Ash

Moisture affects shelf life, flowability, and microbial risk; ash reflects inorganic residues and can indicate how well the raw material was cleaned. Both are routine tests that belong on every well-built COA — if they are missing, ask why.

Heavy Metals

Typical panels cover lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), and arsenic (As), reported in ppm or mg/kg. These limits are not universal: they vary by market, product category, and the finished product's intended daily intake. A limit acceptable in one jurisdiction may fail in another, and food or cosmetic applications may follow different rules than dietary supplements. Always check the limits for your target market and compare them against the COA results instead of assuming a single global standard.

Microbiology

Common microbiological tests on botanical extracts include:

  • Total Plate Count (TPC): An indicator of overall microbial load.
  • Yeast and Mold: Separate counts for fungi.
  • E. coli: Often tested as an indicator organism; some programs also test for specific pathogenic strains.
  • Salmonella: Typically tested as absent in a defined quantity.

Report micro results as numbers (colony-forming units per gram), not just "pass" — acceptable limits, as with heavy metals, depend on your market and application.

Pesticide Residues and Residual Solvents

Pesticide residues and residual solvents are often tested by third-party laboratories, and they may be reported on a separate page or a separate certificate. The COA should indicate which panel was run and what the results were.

For pesticide residues, the relevant limits depend on the market and the specific compound. For residual solvents, limits are usually tied to the extraction process and to the classification of the solvent used. If your extract is water-extracted, residual solvent testing may be less relevant; if ethanol or another organic solvent is used, it is a reasonable item to request.

Batch Number, Dates, and QA Sign-Off

Every COA should include:

  • A batch or lot number that matches your order documents
  • Manufacturing date and expiry or retest date
  • Name and signature of the person who approved the release
  • Date of approval

A COA without a batch number, a signature, or a date is incomplete. These items are not formalities; they are what tie the document to a physical lot and to an accountable person.

How to Spot Red Flags

Watch for these issues:

  • Missing batch number. Without it, the document cannot be tied to your shipment.
  • No test method listed. A result without a method is difficult to interpret or compare.
  • "Complies" without numbers. Statements such as "conforms" or "passes" without the actual result give you nothing to verify.
  • Results that look copy-pasted across batches. Identical values to several decimal places on different batch numbers are unusual and deserve a question.
  • No signature or date. An unsigned, undated COA has no clear accountability.
  • COA for a different product than the invoice. Mismatched names, species, or extract ratios are a serious red flag. Confirm that the COA description matches your purchase order exactly.
  • Specification limits missing. A result is hard to judge if the document does not show the range it was compared against.

None of these issues automatically means the material is bad. But each one weakens the document's value and should prompt a follow-up.

How Buyers Verify a COA

Reading the COA is the first step; verifying it is the second. A few practical approaches:

  1. Ask for the testing method reference. For many botanicals, pharmacopoeia methods (USP, EP, or other recognized compendia) exist — compare the cited method against what your own or a contract lab would use.
  2. Ask whether testing was done in-house or by an independent accredited lab. Both can be valid, but the answer reveals something about the supplier's capabilities and the result's independence; if an external lab was used, ask for its name and accreditation scope.
  3. Request a COA for a recent batch. A COA from two years ago does not describe the material you are buying today. Ask for documentation tied to the batch that will actually ship.
  4. Cross-check limits against your target market. Heavy-metal, microbiological, pesticide, and solvent limits vary by jurisdiction and product category. Confirm what applies to your market and compare the COA results against those limits, not against a generic assumption.
  5. Consider your own verification testing. Many buyers, especially those with mature supplier programs, periodically send samples to an independent lab to confirm that incoming material matches the COA. This is a normal part of supplier management.

Questions to Ask Your Supplier

  • Can you provide a COA for the specific batch that will ship to us?
  • Which analytical methods were used for the assay, and can you share the method reference?
  • Was testing performed in-house or by an independent accredited laboratory?
  • Can you provide the specification sheet that the COA results were compared against?
  • Are pesticide and residual solvent results available, and were they tested by a third party?
  • What is the manufacturing date and the recommended retest or expiry date for this batch?
  • Who approves the COA, and how can we contact the QA function if we have follow-up questions?

A COA is a working document. Read it critically, verify what matters, and keep batch numbers and results on file for every shipment — that habit catches many problems early, when they are still easy to resolve.
