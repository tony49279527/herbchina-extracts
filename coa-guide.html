How to Read a Botanical Extract COA

A Certificate of Analysis is the one document that separates a real extract supplier from a storyteller. Here's how to read one.

HerbChina Extracts · Resources

A COA (Certificate of Analysis) reports the test results for one specific batch of product. If a supplier can't produce one — or the one they show raises more questions than it answers — that's your signal.

1. Product identity

The top of the COA should state the botanical (Latin) name, the plant part used (root, leaf, fruit…), and the extract ratio (e.g. 10:1). A ratio alone means little: 10:1 of what, from which part, standardized to which compound? Identity first, numbers second.

2. Active compounds

Look for the marker compound(s), their content percentage, and — critically — the test method (HPLC, UV, GC, titration). The same extract can read very differently under different methods, so a number without a method is not a specification.

3. Physical characteristics

Appearance (color, powder form), mesh size, loss on drying, and ash content. These tell you whether the powder will behave on your production line — and whether batches are consistent with each other.

4. Heavy metals

Lead (Pb), arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg), each with a result and a limit. Limits differ by market — USP, EU, and Chinese GB standards don't all agree — so check the COA's limits against your market's requirements, not just the supplier's.

5. Pesticide residues

A serious COA screens a pesticide panel and states the method (GC-MS or LC-MS). "Complies" with no panel, no method, and no numbers is a claim, not a result.

6. Microbiology

Total plate count, yeast and mold, and pathogens (E. coli, Salmonella). Again: results and limits, and limits matched to your market.

7. Batch traceability

Every COA must tie to a batch or lot number, with a manufacture date and shelf life. A "typical" COA with no batch number describes a product in theory — ask for the batch-specific one before you commit.

Red flags

  • No test methods listed anywhere on the document.
  • A ratio (10:1, 20:1) with no stated active compound.
  • Results that exactly equal the limits, batch after batch.
  • No batch number, no dates — a "template" COA.
  • Limits that don't match your destination market's regulations.

What to ask your supplier

  1. Can I see a batch-specific COA, not a template?
  2. Which test method was used for the active compound?
  3. Are the heavy metal and pesticide limits set to my market's standards?
  4. Can an independent third-party lab verify a batch before I order?
